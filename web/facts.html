Who's on the pond?

The swans, geese, ducks and coots on Toy City's water aren't placed at random. Which birds a pond gets comes from the Dutch national bird survey. Here are the numbers, where they come from, and the one step we had to estimate ourselves.

How common each bird is

Sovon, the Dutch bird-monitoring organisation, divides the country into squares of . In the survey, volunteers checked every square for breeding birds. This is the share of squares where each species breeds.

Your pond, your chances

A bird breeding somewhere in a 5 × 5 km square doesn't mean it's on every pond in it. Bigger water holds more birds, and a narrow ditch fewer. Slide to see the chance of meeting each bird on a pond of that size, as the game calculates it.

What's measured and what's ours

Measured (Sovon): the percentages of atlas squares, from each species' page in the Sovon Vogelatlas, and the national breeding-pair estimates from the Sovon species statistics.

Our estimate: how a square's chance carries over to one pond. In the game, chance on a pond = share of squares where it breeds × area ÷ (area + m²). So a pond of m² (about 55 × 55 m) gets half its square's chance, and a big lake nearly all of it. Water smaller than m² gets no birds. The survey doesn't measure single ponds, so this step is a reasonable guess, not survey data.

Our choice: how many birds come together (a swan pair, sometimes with cygnets; geese in groups), how they look (toy models, not field-guide drawings), and that the game uses the breeding-season numbers all year round.

Everyone in a town sees the same birds: which birds a pond has, and where they swim, follow from the pond and the clock, the same way the town's traffic does.
